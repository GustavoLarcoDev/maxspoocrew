What to look for, in order

First, the headline — it is the only thing that gets read from three feet away, so if two flyers have interchangeable headlines one of them is wasted. Second, whether the piece commits: a flyer that argues price and health and trust argues nothing. Third, what the person holding it was doing thirty seconds earlier, which is what actually decides whether M beats H on a given noticeboard.

Three plain ones

Picture, logo, one line

The ten above argue. These three do not. A photograph, the logo, one sentence, the phone number and a QR — nothing else on the page. That is the right piece for a noticeboard read at ten feet, a shop window, or a stack by a till where nobody is going to stand and read paragraphs.

The photographs are generated artwork and contain no lettering at all, which is deliberate: every word here is real type, so a price can change without regenerating an image. The artwork prints at about 176 dpi across the sheet.

The same ten, half the width

Rack cards — 3.75 × 8.25 in

The same ten arguments on a tall, narrow card. This is the shape that fits a countertop rack, a vet’s brochure holder, a mailer envelope or a back pocket — and it is the one to hand somebody who is standing up.

It holds about a third of the copy a letter flyer does, which is the whole design constraint. Each card keeps one idea and stops. The comparison card gave up its table entirely, because three columns of ticks across 3.15in of live width is not a table, it is an eye test — it runs as three stacked blocks instead.

Before printing

Six things worth knowing

The slogan on the older pieces was wrong, and is now fixed

Three pieces — the 18 × 12 door magnet and the two AI advertisements — were printed with “No pile left behind”. That is not Max’s Poo Crew’s slogan; it was written for these mockups and it paraphrases a competitor’s line closely enough to be a problem on a vehicle. All three now carry the real one from the website, “We scoop it. You enjoy it.” If you have already printed any of those three, they are the ones to reprint.

All twenty are editable

Every piece here is generated from HTML in print/src/. The letter flyers share _flyer.css, the rack cards share _rack.css, and both sit on the same _base.css — which is why twenty pieces arguing ten things still look like one company. Run python print/build.py and every PDF and preview on this page rebuilds. Flyers A through G, plus the two built on generated artwork, live over on the main print page.

Every price on these sheets is printed, not linked

Twenty-three pieces now carry rates in ink. If the rates on the website change, they have to change in the sources too or the printed material starts lying — H, I, K, L, O, P and Q in both sizes, plus the three plain ones. The QR looks after itself.

The QR codes are tagged, and now they are checked

Each format scans through to maxspoocrew.com carrying its own utm_source — flyer, rack, trifold, card and so on — so Analytics can tell you which piece is actually earning the scans rather than just that print is working. python print/checkqr.py decodes the code out of every finished PDF and fails if one stops scanning or loses its tag; a QR that does not read is not something you discover on screen.

Pick a size before you pick a design

The two sets are not interchangeable. A letter flyer is for a surface somebody is standing in front of — a corkboard, a counter, a door. A rack card is for a holder, a pocket or an envelope, and it is what you hand to a person. If the piece has to be taken away, it wants to be the rack card.

Do not print all twenty

Pick two or three per size, matched to where they are actually going, and print those in volume. Twenty designs at a hundred copies each costs far more per sheet than four at five hundred, and sixteen of them will sit in a box. The point of having twenty is choosing, not distributing.
